What is happening?

  1. State: amplitudes describe a maze cell and one of four directional ports: right, down, left, up. Start at S with equal port amplitudes.
  2. Coin: at each cell, the four-port Grover coin maps amplitude a to 2 × the local port mean − a. This is a small mixing operation, not the earlier global Grover route search.
  3. Shift: move each port amplitude across its open edge into the opposite port. A blocked port stays at its cell. Opposite amplitudes can reinforce or cancel in the next coin step.
  4. Detector: after each shift, measure only “at G or elsewhere?” Detected probability is removed and accumulated. The remaining branch keeps its coherence. No intermediate full-position measurements are made.
  5. Classical baseline: replace amplitudes by probabilities and remove phase coherence after every operation. Since every squared coin entry is ¼, choose each port with probability ¼; blocked choices stay put. Apply exactly the same exit detector.

This measures arrival probability, not a recovered route. A position measurement does not reveal the walk's history. BFS returns the actual shortest route. No plan enumeration or valid-route oracle is used here; both walks know the grid.

Where quantum advantage can occur
There are proven quantum-walk speedups for specially constructed black-box graph problems. That result does not establish an advantage on an ordinary known grid. This report tests a particular four-port walk with reflecting walls and repeated exit detection; it is not a reproduction of a welded-tree algorithm or a stationary-state maze path-extraction algorithm.
References: Childs et al.: exponential algorithmic speedup by quantum walk; Matsuoka et al.: maze solving using quantum walks. The latter uses a different absorbing-hole/self-loop construction.
